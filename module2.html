Module 2 of the VHDO Training Curriculum · Foundations of Opticianry & Medical Basics

Welcome to Visual Health Doctors of Optometry. If you've never worked in optical before, that's fine: almost everyone here started exactly where you are. This course covers the basics: how the eye works, how to read a prescription, what the lens and frame terms mean, and how to explain all of it to patients in plain English. Work through it in your first week. You're not expected to memorize it. You're expected to know where to look things up and to ask when you're unsure. Guessing in front of a patient is the only real mistake.

Chapter 1

How the Eye Works (and What Goes Wrong)

Think of the eye as a camera. Light enters through the cornea (the clear front window), passes through the pupil, gets focused by the lens inside the eye, and lands on the retina at the back, the "film" that sends the image to the brain. For clear vision, light has to land exactly on the retina. When it doesn't, that's a refractive error, and that's what glasses and contacts fix.

The Four Common Refractive Errors

Myopia (nearsighted). The eye is slightly too long, so light focuses in front of the retina. Distance is blurry, near is clear. Corrected with minus lenses (e.g., −3.00). These patients take their glasses off to read a phone.

Hyperopia (farsighted). The eye is slightly too short, so light focuses behind the retina. Young patients can often force it clear by straining, which causes headaches and fatigue. Corrected with plus lenses (e.g., +2.00).

Astigmatism. The cornea is shaped more like a football than a basketball, so light focuses at two points instead of one. Everything looks a bit blurry or "stretched" at all distances. Corrected with cylinder power at a specific axis.

Presbyopia. Not a defect, just aging. Around 40-45 the lens inside the eye stiffens and can't flex to focus up close, so arms get "too short." Corrected with an ADD power: readers, bifocals, or progressives. Everyone gets it eventually.

Say it to a patient

"Nearsighted means you see well up close but not far away. Farsighted is the opposite. Astigmatism means your eye's front surface is shaped a little oval instead of round. And presbyopia is the reading-glasses thing that happens to all of us in our 40s."

Reading a Prescription

PD, the Measurement That Makes or Breaks the Job

PD is the distance between pupil centers in millimeters. The optical center of the lens must sit in front of the pupil; get it wrong and the patient gets unwanted prism: eyestrain, headaches, "these just feel off." A single PD is one number (63). A dual (monocular) PD measures each eye from the nose center (31.5 / 32), which is more accurate, because faces aren't symmetrical. Always use monocular PD for progressives and strong Rx's. Near PD runs about 3 mm smaller, since the eyes converge to read.

Chapter 3

Measurements You'll Take

PD. Use the pupillometer. Have the patient look at the distance target, not at you. Double-check against the doctor's records or the old glasses if something seems off.

Seg height / fitting height. Measured after the frame is chosen and adjusted on the face. Mark the pupil center (progressives) or lower lid margin (bifocals) on the demo lens, then measure down to the deepest part of the lens groove. Wrong seg height is the #1 cause of progressive remakes.

Frame measurements. Stamped inside the temple: 52□18-140 = 52 eye size (lens width, mm), 18 bridge (DBL), 140 temple length. For strong Rx's, two more matter: B measurement (lens height) and ED (widest diagonal).

Pantoscopic tilt. How far the frame front tilts in toward the cheeks. About 8-12° is normal and helps reading through a progressive.

Vertex distance. The gap from lens back to cornea, usually 12-14 mm. Above roughly ±4.00, moving the lens changes effective power, which is why contacts and glasses numbers differ, and why a strong Rx shouldn't slide down the nose.

Chapter 4

Lens Designs

Single vision: one power across the whole lens. Simplest and cheapest. Bifocal (FT-28): distance on top, reading in a D-shaped segment, visible line, image "jump," nothing for arm's length. Trifocal: adds an intermediate band and two lines; mostly long-time wearers. Progressive (PAL): power increases gradually down the lens, giving no line, no jump, and every distance. The trade-off is soft peripheral zones in the lower outer corners. That's physics, not a defect, and better digital designs give wider corridors.

PatientUsually best
Under 40, no ADDSingle vision
First-time presbyope, activeProgressive
Budget-driven, no computer useBifocal FT-28
Heavy desk/computer userProgressive + dedicated computer pair
Failed progressives beforeBifocal, or better PAL design with careful measurement

Explaining progressives to a first-timer

"These give you clear vision at every distance with no line. Two things to know: point your nose at what you want to see, and the bottom outer corners will be soft, because that area isn't meant for looking through. Most people adapt in a few days to a week. Wear them all day from the start, don't switch back to your old pair, and on stairs look down with your chin. If you're still fighting them after two weeks, come see us and we'll check the fit."

Rules of thumb. Kids, monocular patients (one good eye), and anyone doing sports get polycarbonate or Trivex, no exceptions. Rimless and drill-mounts need Trivex or poly; CR-39 and high-index crack at the drill holes. Stronger Rx → higher index → more reflections, so pair high-index with AR every time.

Say it to a patient

"For your prescription, this thinner material takes a good chunk off the edge thickness and cuts the weight, so your lenses sit closer to the frame instead of sticking out past it."

Chapter 6

Treatments and Coatings

Anti-reflective (AR). The most valuable upgrade there is. Removes surface reflections: better night driving, less screen fatigue, and people see the patient's eyes instead of glare. Premium AR adds smudge-resistant layers.

Scratch coating. Standard now. Scratch-resistant, never scratch-proof. Don't promise otherwise.

UV protection. Built into poly, Trivex, and high-index; added as a treatment on CR-39.

Photochromic (Transitions). Clear indoors, dark in sunlight. Key limitation: most don't darken inside a car, because the windshield blocks the UV that triggers them. Slower in cold weather too.

Polarized. Kills glare off flat surfaces: roads, water, snow. Best for driving, fishing, skiing. Always a tint; never goes clear. Can make some LCD screens hard to read.

Blue-light filtering. Patients ask constantly. Be honest: the evidence is about comfort and glare, not disease prevention.

Say it to a patient

AR: "It gets the glare out of the lens so you see better at night and people see your eyes, not the reflection." · Photochromic: "Clear inside, sunglasses outside. Just know the windshield keeps most of them from getting dark in the car." · Blue light: "A lot of people find it makes long screen days more comfortable. I wouldn't promise it does more than that."

Chapter 7

Frame Basics

Parts: front (eyewire/rims), bridge, nose pads, endpieces, hinges, temples, temple tips. You'll use these names with the lab and with patients.

Materials. Zyl/acetate: rich colors, adjusts with heat. Metal: thin profile, adjustable nose pads; titanium is light, strong, and hypoallergenic (nickel-sensitive patients). TR-90/nylon: flexible and near-indestructible, which makes it the pick for kids and sports. Rimless: minimal look, but requires poly or Trivex and a modest Rx.

Fit. Width matches the face (temples don't bow out), bridge rests evenly, front sits level with eyes slightly above center, temples bend down behind the ear, lashes clear the lenses.

Adjustments. Heat acetate before bending, because cold acetate snaps. Adjust metal at the endpiece and temple, never the eyewire. One side at a time; re-check level after every change. When you don't know how, get someone who does. A broken frame costs more than a question.

Matching frame to Rx: where opticians earn their keep. Strong minus = thick edges → smaller, rounder, full-rim frame + high-index. Strong plus = thick center → smaller frames again. A well-centered frame (frame PD close to patient PD) is a thinner lens. Progressives need enough B measurement, generally 28 mm+, or the reading zone won't fit.

Say it to a patient

"With a prescription like yours, this smaller frame will give you a noticeably thinner, lighter lens than that big one: same look you want, half the edge."

Chapter 8

Contact Lens Basics

You won't be fitting contacts, but you'll answer questions about them daily.

Soft vs. RGP. Soft lenses (hydrogel/silicone hydrogel) are comfortable almost immediately, which is why they account for the vast majority of wearers. RGP lenses are small and firm: sharper optics, great for high astigmatism and irregular corneas, weeks to adapt.

Schedules. Dailies: new pair every day, no cleaning, healthiest, highest yearly cost. Bi-weekly/monthly: cleaned nightly; cheaper only if the patient actually follows the schedule (most over-wear them).

Types. Sphere for plain near/farsighted. Toric for astigmatism, weighted to stay oriented and needing more fitting time. Multifocal for presbyopia; monovision is the alternative.

Why a CL Rx differs from glasses: ① vertex distance, since the lens sits on the eye rather than 12 mm away, which means strong powers are recalculated (−6.00 glasses ≈ −5.50 contacts); ② base curve and diameter, fit parameters glasses don't have; ③ it's written for a specific brand and isn't interchangeable. A glasses Rx runs 1-2 years; a CL Rx is usually 1 year.

Why the fitting fee: say it like this

"The exam gives us your prescription. The contact lens fitting is the extra visit where the doctor makes sure the lens physically fits your eye safely and that the vision is right. It's a separate service, so there's a separate fee. Your insurance may cover part of it."

Chapter 9

Vision Insurance 101

Vision plans are not medical insurance, and that gap is the single biggest source of patient confusion. A vision plan covers routine care: the annual exam plus a materials benefit toward frames and lenses. Think of it as a discount/allowance program with predictable caps. Medical insurance covers eye health problems such as pink eye, dry eye, glaucoma, diabetic exams, and injuries, all billed with a diagnosis code against the medical deductible. A patient can use both.

Plans you'll see: VSP, EyeMed, Davis Vision, Spectera/UnitedHealthcare Vision, Superior Vision. Each has its own portal, allowances, and covered-lens rules. Part 2 covers which ones VHDO bills and how.

Worked example. $10 exam copay + $25 materials copay. Frame allowance $150; the frame is $220, so the $70 overage gets a 20% discount → $56. Basic single-vision lenses covered after the copay; AR add-on at the plan price, say $45. Total: $10 + $25 + $56 + $45 = $136.

Always verify eligibility before the patient starts choosing, and quote the out-of-pocket total before you order. Surprise balances at pickup are the fastest way to lose a patient.

Chapter 10

Professionalism and Compliance

HIPAA in One Page

Rule of thumb: information about a patient belongs to that patient.

Do: angle screens away from the waiting area, lock your workstation, lower your voice discussing an Rx or balance at the desk, shred anything with patient info, verify who you're speaking to on the phone, access only what your job requires.

Don't: confirm or deny that someone is a patient to anyone, including a spouse or parent of an adult child, without authorization. No hallway or break-room patient talk, no photos, no personal text/email for patient info, and never look up friends, family, or yourself out of curiosity. That's the most common way people get fired.

If information gets disclosed by mistake, tell your manager immediately. Fast reporting is treated very differently from a discovered cover-up.

Handling an Unhappy Patient

  1. Listen all the way through: don't interrupt, don't defend.
  2. Acknowledge: "I'm sorry. That's frustrating." (A feeling, not fault.)
  3. Ask one clarifying question.
  4. Move them out of the lobby if voices rise.
  5. Fix what you can right now: adjust, recheck, remake, pull the order.
  6. Escalate early: refunds and clinical questions go to a manager or the doctor. Nobody is annoyed that you asked.
  7. Follow up in a couple of days. It turns complaints into loyal patients surprisingly often.

Never argue about whether the patient is right. Focus entirely on what happens next.
