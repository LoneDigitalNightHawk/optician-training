Part Two of the VHDO Training Manual

Part 1 taught you optical in general. This part teaches you this practice. Everything in these chapters comes from VHDO's own sales records — 6,069 line items across all 11 offices for the pay period of 13–25 July 2026, plus the full item-level catalog from May 2026. Where you see a percentage or a dollar figure, it describes what we actually sold, not what an optical textbook says a practice sells.

In that two-week window the company did $590,197 net across 1,215 orders — an average order value of $526. Hold onto that number. It's the bar.

One habit to build now: when a product name appears in this style below, that is the exact wording you'll see on the screen in Eyefinity. Learn to recognise them by sight.

Exams and contact lenses are two-thirds of the business. All spectacle materials together — frames, lenses, coatings and every upgrade — come to about 28.6%. VHDO is an exam-and-contact-lens practice first and a dispensary second. That shapes your day: the patient in front of you is far more often here for an exam or a contact lens supply than to browse frames.

Two more numbers worth knowing on day one. Returns and reversals are 7.7% of gross — about $49,000 in two weeks; careful measurement is revenue, not paperwork. And order value varies enormously by type: a contact lens order averages $850, a spectacle order $635, an exam that converts to materials $550, and an exam where the patient leaves with nothing $298.

Stay honest — Part 1, Chapter 6 set the standard and it holds:

Dailies vs monthlies, in plain language

"Dailies are a fresh pair every morning — nothing to clean, nothing to store, and they're the healthiest option. Monthlies cost less over a year, but only if you're genuinely good about cleaning them every night."

Most people over-wear monthlies; say so kindly.

Chapter 17

Insurance at VHDO

A necessary correction to Part 1

Chapter 9 of Part 1 lists VSP, EyeMed, Davis Vision, Spectera/UnitedHealthcare and Superior Vision as "the big national vision plans" and says Part 2 will cover which ones VHDO is in-network with. It cannot, and this chapter does not. No carrier or plan name exists anywhere in VHDO's exported sales, production, receivables or daily-closing data — insurance appears only as dollar amounts. That national list is plausible for Northern Virginia but it is not sourced from VHDO's own records, and nobody should present it to a patient as our network until billing confirms it.

Ask your manager or the billing team for the current in-network list, or read it off the Eyefinity/OfficeMate claim screens, on your first day. Everything else in this chapter is measured from our real numbers and you can rely on it.

How much of our business runs through a plan. Insurance touches 53.8% of line items but 71.9% of revenue — just over half the tickets, nearly three-quarters of the money.

The most useful number in this manual: on an average $526 order, the patient pays about $278 and insurance pays about $248. Across the period that's $338,335 in patient responsibility against $301,013 in net insurance receivable. You will collect from the person standing in front of you roughly as often as you collect from a plan. Anyone who thinks of insurance as "the thing that pays" has it half wrong.

Read the bottom two rows again. Premium lens designs, photochromics and polarized are overwhelmingly out-of-pocket conversations. Only one in seven photochromic or polarized lines has any insurance on it at all. That is precisely where a trained recommendation, rather than a benefit table, decides the outcome.

How a typical insured sale flows. The plan sets an allowance (its own price for the item), we write the difference from retail off as contractual expense, the patient pays a copay plus any balance, and the remainder becomes net insurance receivable we bill and collect later.

Worked Example

Established patient, exam plus a single-pair spectacle order, using real averages from the period:

ServiceRetailPlan allowanceCopayPatient balance
92014 Comprehensive, existing$226.00$223.06$3.80$6.75

Patient owes about $43 for the visit — and notice its shape. The refraction is effectively fully covered (four cents) and the comprehensive exam leaves about $10, so almost the entire $43 is the retinal screening, which the plan values at $22.43 against our $49. Retinal photos are an elective offer, not a covered benefit. Present them that way and quote the ~$30.

At the dispensary, a spectacle order averages $635. Using our average patient responsibility per line — frame ~$143, lens base ~$60, coating ~$48, three add-on lines ~$96 combined — the patient's share lands near $347 and insurance covers the balance.

The rule that follows from all of this: verify eligibility before the patient starts choosing, and quote the out-of-pocket total with insurance already applied before you place the order. A patient who has heard the real number early will buy. A patient who discovers it at pickup becomes part of our 7.7% return rate.

Five Habits to Build in Your First Month

  1. Finish the visit. 57% of our exam patients leave with nothing — 535 of 935 exam orders. An exam-only ticket averages $298; one that converts averages $635. Every conversion is worth about $336. Walk the patient to the frame board yourself; don't hand them off to the door.
  2. Offer a second pair. Every time. Our multi-pair rate for the period was 0.0% — 400 spectacle orders, 400 single pairs. Not one backup, computer pair or prescription sunglass across 11 offices in two weeks. This is the single largest untapped line in the business, and it is wide open.
  3. Never skip AR. 91.8% is the house standard. Blue-light AR is 41% of all AR we sell — TechShield Blue UVR and Crizal Prevencia UV are your defaults, not a stretch.
  4. Quote with insurance already applied, before they choose. The patient pays roughly $278 of a $526 order. Tell them their number early and the sale holds together.
  5. Price the contact lens fitting before the fitting. A complex fit runs the patient $115–$129 out of pocket. Surprise at checkout is where these orders die.

And a sixth, quietly worth as much as any of them: measure carefully. Returns are 7.7% of gross. A remake costs the practice more than most upgrades earn.

Chapter 19

Why Glasses Pay the Bills

Chapter 12 told you exams and contact lenses are two-thirds of our revenue. This chapter tells you why eyeglasses are where the profit is — and why your job, above every other habit in this manual, is to sell more of them.

Our sales export doesn't show cost of goods, but the economics of an optical practice are no secret, and they run like this:

  • Exams are capacity-limited. A doctor can only see so many patients a day, and when insurance is involved the price is fixed by the plan's allowance — a $226 exam pays what it pays. You cannot grow exam revenue without more doctor-hours.
  • Contact lenses are mostly cost of goods. The boxes come from the same manufacturers that supply every online retailer, and patients can — and do — price-shop them. An $850 CL order is big on the ticket and thin underneath it.
  • Eyeglasses are the opposite of both. Frames and lenses carry the highest gross margin in the practice, every upgrade on the lens ladder is nearly pure margin, and — this is the part that matters — a pair of glasses consumes zero additional doctor time. The exam already happened. The dispensary is the only part of this business that can grow without another patient in the chair.

So when spectacle materials show up as "only" 28.6% of revenue, read that as headroom, not as unimportance. Every practice metric in this chapter exists to move that number up.

What the Upside Actually Looks Like

In our two-week window, 935 orders contained an exam and only 42.6% of them converted into any materials — glasses or contacts. At our best associate's capture rate of 63.2%, roughly 190 more of those same exams would have become materials sales. At ~$336 per conversion, that's about $65,000 more per pay period — over $1.6 million a year — from the patients we already examined. No new marketing, no extra doctor-hours, no additional chair time. Just finishing more visits.

That is why capture is the first number on the dashboard below, and why every strategy in this chapter points at the same outcome: the exam patient leaves with glasses.

The Playbook — How You Move Those Numbers

  1. Sell the prescription, not the shelf. The strongest reason to buy glasses walks out of the exam room holding it: "The doctor updated your prescription — let's get your lenses caught up to it." A changed Rx is a doctor's recommendation to purchase; deliver it that way. This is the capture play, and it's why the warm handoff from exam room to dispensary matters more than any closing technique.
  2. Build the lenses first, then the frame. Our best spectacle jobs are materials-weighted: design, material, coating chosen deliberately, then the frame. Patients who pick a frame first anchor on the frame price and fight every lens line afterward. Lens-first is how a $463 job becomes a $776 job — that's the spectacle-AOV play.
  3. Quote with insurance already applied — early. The patient pays about $278 of the average order. Give them their real number before they've fallen in love or walked away from a sticker price that isn't theirs. Fear of an unknown total kills more glasses sales than any competitor does.
  4. Offer the second pair at the moment of highest intent. Right when the first pair is decided — not at pickup, not "next time." Prescription sunglasses and a computer pair are the two natural offers: "Your frame benefit is used on this pair, so a second pair is out-of-pocket either way — today I can build your sun pair on the same Rx while everything's measured and fresh." The company rate is 0.0%; any yes you get is new money.
  5. Recommend photochromic or polarized once a day, every day. At 17% and 6% of lenses — and with only one line in seven carrying any insurance — these sales belong entirely to whoever recommends them. That's you.
  6. Pull your own numbers weekly. Capture, spectacle AOV, frame attach, coating attach, second pairs offered. Compare against the table above and against Chapter 18's leaders. What gets measured moves; what gets measured by you moves faster.

Say it to a patient

"The doctor changed your prescription today, so your current lenses are officially out of date. Let's build your new pair while your measurements are fresh — and since your frame benefit covers this one, I'll show you what a prescription sun pair runs as a second pair before you decide."

None of this is pressure selling. Every play above starts from something true — the prescription changed, the benefit exists, the sun pair solves a real problem — said clearly, at the right moment, by someone who knows the numbers. That's the whole difference between a $298 visit and a $635 one.
